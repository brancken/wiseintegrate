AI can help a church with the work that fills the week without needing pastoral judgment: emails, scheduling, rosters, event planning and visitor follow-up. It should never preach, counsel, interpret Scripture or handle members' personal information. The churches that use it well start with clear boundaries and written guidelines, then begin with one small, low-risk task.

## Many leaders use AI, few churches have guidelines

Between November 2025 and December 2025, Barna and Pushpay surveyed 1,306 church leaders for their State of Church Tech 2026 report. According to [Barna's summary](https://www.barna.com/research/church-leaders-ai-usage-concerns/):

| Finding | Result |
|---|---|
| Church leaders who use AI at least once a month | a majority |
| Churches using AI in any part of their ministry or operations | 33% |
| Churches that have AI guidelines | 5% |

An earlier Pushpay survey of about 1,700 church leaders, in February 2025, found that 45% of church leaders were using AI, as [MinistryWatch reported](https://ministrywatch.com/ai-in-church-operations-shifts-from-early-adopter-to-mainstream/).

The gap is the point. Many leaders already use AI on their own, but very few churches have agreed together how it should and should not be used. That is where to start.

## Start with boundaries, not tools

Before anyone opens an AI tool on behalf of the church, agree on what AI will never do. These are the boundaries we ask every church we work with to agree to:

- AI will never preach, teach Scripture, or exercise pastoral authority.
- AI will never provide pastoral counselling, spiritual direction, or crisis care.
- AI will never interpret Scripture or produce theological doctrine.
- AI will never be presented as a spiritual guide to your congregation.
- No member's personal or pastoral information ever enters an AI system.
- Every AI output is reviewed by a human before it is used.

Write these down and have your leadership agree to them. That one page is already more than most churches have.

## Where AI can help a church

The useful places are the tasks that take your team's time without needing pastoral judgment.

### Administration and communication

- Drafting routine emails, notices and newsletters for a person to check and send.
- Turning meeting notes into a clear list of actions.
- Scheduling and staff communication.

### Ministry operations

- Volunteer coordination and rosters.
- Event planning checklists.
- Visitor follow-up and care workflows, where the AI helps with the process but never sees members' private details.

### Outreach

- Social media posts and website updates.
- Translating notices for people in your community who speak another language.

In each case a person reads the result before it is used. AI can sound confident and still be wrong.

## What to keep away from AI

Some things are easy to forget:

- **Prayer requests and pastoral notes.** These are personal and pastoral information. They stay out of AI tools.
- **Member lists with private details.** Keep names, phone numbers, birthdays and family situations out of AI chats.
- **Anything theological.** AI makes too many theological errors to be trusted with teaching or doctrine.

## Writing your church's AI guidelines

A short, plain document is enough. Cover:

1. **Who may use AI for church work**, and for which tasks.
2. **The boundaries** above, word for word if they fit your church.
3. **What information may never be entered**, with examples.
4. **The review rule**: who checks AI output before it is used.
5. **Being honest**: when and how you tell people that AI helped with something.
6. **A review date**, so the guidelines are looked at again as tools change.

Keep it to one or two pages. A document people actually read is better than a long policy nobody opens.

## Start small

Pick one task that is low risk and takes real time each week, such as drafting the weekly notices for a person to edit. Try it for a month. Ask the people involved whether it saved time and whether anything felt wrong. Only then add a second task.
